V.H. Belvadi's theme for the January 2025 [IndieWeb Carnival](https://indieweb.org/IndieWeb_Carnival), [*On the importance of friction*](https://vhbelvadi.com/indieweb-carnival-friction), is an invitation for me to gather various threads that I have found in my reading in the last few weeks, and indeed to practise that friction in writing this post.[^delay]

[^delay]: I'm actually typing up this post *twelve days* after writing it down on paper, even missing the [early deadline](https://vhbelvadi.com/indieweb-carnival-roundup-2025). It doesn't usually take me that long, but it's been Quite A Month, and a busy contrast to the relaxing couple of weeks around the New Year.

As I slowed down over the New Year and spent more time on slow and deep reading, I saw that other bloggers were also developing more intentional reading practices. [Jeremy Friesen](https://takeonrules.com/2024/12/31/here-i-think/) customised his Mastodon and Emacs setup,

> to reduce doom-scrolling opportunities ... I created a process to see one post at a time. This is done by fetching 50 then narrowing to show one at a time.
>
> When I get to the end of the 50 posts, I call it good enough.

[Tracy Durnell](https://tracydurnell.com/2025/01/04/disrupting-my-reading-habits/) observed that

> Information acts as a modern enchantment, keeping us in its sway. ... I want to replace my “mental junk food” binge habit with more thoughtful reading.

At the same time, I've been seeing more references in wider media to the notion of "brain rot", initially as a metaphor -- akin to "doomscrolling" -- for patterns of reading and media consumption encouraged by predatory social media systems. There is even early research suggesting that "brain rot" might be physical, not just metaphorical.[^rot] And in the *Financial Times*, {{< archive-robustlink text="Mia Levitin" originalurl="https://www.ft.com/content/fe9963aa-f4fd-4839-8732-5df7bc4317bb" versionurl="https://archive.md/IcUcI" versiondate="2025-01-02" >}} quotes Shakespeare scholar Jonathan Bate:

> Forty years ago "you could say to a student, 'This week it's Dickens. Please read Great Expectations, David Copperfield and Bleak House' ... Now, instead of three novels in a week, many students will struggle to get through one novel in three weeks."

[^rot]: Frank Landymore, "['Brain Rot' Is Poisoning Our Minds](https://futurism.com/neoscope/brain-rot-poisoning-minds)", *Futurism*, 28 December, 2024; Michoel L. Moshel et al., "[Neuropsychological Deficits in Disordered Screen Use Behaviours: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11065-023-09612-4)", *Neuropsychology Review* 34, no. 3 (September 2024): 791--822.

As a child of the 1970s and 1980s I have seen the greatly exaggerated news of the death of reading before, as responses to television and video games. But as a child of the 1970s and 1980s who started an English literature degree, I also remember the expectation that we would read a novel each week, for each unit (a full-time load being three or four units), and as a first-year student this felt (initially) challenging but not impossible.[^history]

[^history]: When I switched to history the average was two or three hefty articles or book chapters each week.

I read twelve novels and four non-fiction books in [all of 2024]({{< ref "/reading/#read-in-2024" >}}). Granted, I'm not an undergraduate student any more, but even compared to the years before I started my most recent graduate course (the same month that COVID-19 became a real concern), I was spending less time in deep reading for its own sake. Studying during lockdown induced a years-long brain fog that kept me from leisure reading, but I no longer have the excuse of study or brain fog. I have been, like so many people, spending more time than ever looking at digital screens both at work and away from work, and often scrolling lazily through feeds (whether [RSS]({{< ref "/feeds" >}}) or [Mastodon]({{< ref "/library/fediverse" >}})) instead of actively choosing to read specific books or articles that sit sadly on my bookshelves or in my virtual bookmarks.

In 2024 I did consciously begin to limit my screen time, and while I did not resume my undergraduate reading habits, I did still read more books than in the previous three years combined, and I returned to writing more regularly and posting to this blog more frequently. In fact, my two previous IndieWeb Carnival posts also touched on different aspects of friction (maybe that's a topic that really gets me going). As in ["Building a bliss station"]({{< ref "2024-05-28-building-a-bliss-station" >}}), I'm drafting this post with pencil and paper, to be typed and edited in Vim, then pushed to a git repository and published with the [Hugo](https://gohugo.io/) static site generator. The process comprises about two weeks of ruminating over various themes and finding connections between them (not all posts take this long to gestate), about an hour writing an initial draft, maybe another half hour typing and editing,[^shortcodes] then [a few minutes with `git`, `ssh`, and `hugo`]({{< ref "2024-07-30-indiewebbing-from-the-couch" >}}) on the command line. Writing on paper, editing in Vim, and running terminal commands are all second nature to me as I've been writing and programming like this for more than half my lifetime.

[^shortcodes]: Maybe add another half hour if I also decide to tinker with my site configuration, as I did this time to add a shortcode for IndieWeb Carnival footer information.

I imagine that to someone whose consumption and creation of digital content is primarily through smartphone interfaces, what I just described could sound tedious or plain incomprehensible. But these tools and habits weren't just products of their time (though I would argue that paper and plain text have demonstrated their longevity); they fit the way my brain works. There is joy in the friction of writing slowly, and in typing plain text in a terminal (my digital equivalent of a paper notebook), and seeing the result rendered in HTML and a splash of CSS.

The last few weeks have also seen content creators and ordinary internet users cut off from, or forcing themselves away from, some of the world's largest corporate-owned social media platforms. Some of them have found their way to the fediverse where old hands are putting effort (more so than in previous years) into giving newcomers friendly and helpful guidance on [navigating decentralised social media](https://aus.social/@claudinec/113853760844701777). But for complete newcomers, there is a great deal of friction in choosing fediverse servers and connecting to their friends on other servers. And this friction is small compared to the many steps involved in joining the IndieWeb: "buying" (actually *renting*) your own domain name and choosing both web hosting and software for your blog, newsletter, or video channel. It saddens me but I cannot blame those who give up on the fediverse and turn to Bluesky, or who find the [IndieWeb wiki](https://indieweb.org/) overwhelming. Some friction can be a source of creativity and self-improvement. Too much friction can become an insurmountable barrier. I don't have easy (low-friction) solutions to making everyone feel welcome in the fediverse and IndieWeb; I don't event think those are realistic goals. All I can do is keep talking to new people fleeing corporate platforms and try to learn about each individual newcomer's sources of friction, and see whether these can become creative, not discouraging.
